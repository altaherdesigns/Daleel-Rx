Magnesium supplement shelves are crowded with names — oxide, citrate, glycinate,
chloride — and the differences between them aren't marketing noise. They're mostly about
how well each form actually gets absorbed.

## Why the form matters

The compound magnesium is bound to changes how readily it dissolves and crosses into the
bloodstream. According to NIH's Office of Dietary Supplements, forms that dissolve well in
liquid — aspartate, citrate, lactate, and chloride — tend to have higher bioavailability
than magnesium oxide or magnesium sulfate, even when the oxide form often lists more
elemental magnesium per tablet on the label. A bigger number on the bottle doesn't mean
more of it actually reaches your system.

## What magnesium is doing in the body

Magnesium is involved in several hundred enzymatic reactions — nerve and muscle function,
blood glucose regulation, blood pressure regulation, and protein synthesis among them. Food
sources include green leafy vegetables, legumes, nuts, whole grains, and fish; dietary
intake alone is enough for most people who eat a varied diet.

## Who's actually at risk of a deficiency

Low magnesium status is more of a concern with specific risk factors than in the general
population: gastrointestinal disease, chronic diarrhoea, long-term proton pump inhibitor
use, alcohol use disorder, and diuretic use (both loop and thiazide) are the commonly cited
ones. It's also common to see low magnesium alongside low potassium or calcium — treating
those without correcting magnesium first often doesn't fully resolve them.

## Safety and interactions

There's no established upper limit for magnesium from food, but there is one for
supplemental magnesium specifically, because the main side effect — diarrhoea — is a
dose-related, direct effect of unabsorbed magnesium in the gut. This matters for drug
interactions too: magnesium can reduce the absorption of certain antibiotics
(tetracyclines and fluoroquinolones among them) and bisphosphonates if taken too close
together, which is a timing issue, not a reason to avoid magnesium altogether.

## The practical takeaway

If the goal is correcting a genuine dietary shortfall, form matters less than just getting
enough of it consistently. If the goal is a specific use — sleep, muscle cramps, migraine
prevention — the evidence is far more form- and dose-specific than most product marketing
suggests, and interactions with existing medication are common enough to be worth a quick
check with a pharmacist before starting, not after.
