"Probiotics" on a label covers a lot of ground — the evidence behind that word depends
entirely on which organism, at what dose, for what condition. Treating it as one thing is
where most of the confusion starts.

## What the strain actually means

Probiotic microorganisms are named by genus, species, and strain — for example,
*Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus* GG. A benefit shown for one strain doesn't automatically
apply to a different strain of the same species, let alone a different species entirely.
This is the detail most marketing skips, and it's the one that determines whether a claim
is backed by anything.

## Where the evidence is genuinely strong

Certain *Lactobacillus* strains and *Saccharomyces boulardii* have solid trial evidence
for treating acute infectious diarrhoea and for preventing antibiotic-associated
diarrhoea. This is the area probiotic research is most consistent about.

## Where it's mixed but plausible

For constipation, a 2014 review of 14 studies found some benefit, particularly with
*Bifidobacterium lactis*; a 2017 review in elderly adults found a small but meaningful
effect with *Bifidobacterium longum*. In children, a separate 2017 review of the same
question found no clear benefit — a good example of why results don't transfer cleanly
across age groups.

For inflammatory bowel disease, results are mixed: some evidence of benefit in ulcerative
colitis, none established for Crohn's disease.

## Where the evidence isn't there yet

Weight and body fat are commonly claimed benefits with the weakest support — some
studies show a slight reduction, others show no effect or even a slight increase. NIH's
own consumer fact sheet is blunt about this: more research is needed before probiotics
can be recommended for weight management specifically.

## What's actually worth knowing before buying one

Not every product labelled "probiotic" has been studied — many haven't, so their effects
aren't known. In 2023, the FDA specifically warned against giving probiotics to preterm
infants after they were linked to serious infections and at least one death; probiotics
haven't gone through FDA's premarket safety and efficacy review the way medications have.
In healthy adults, the main downside is usually just mild gas — but "generally safe for
healthy adults" and "proven to do what the label says" are two different claims, and only
one of them is well established.

## The practical takeaway

If you're recommending or taking a probiotic for a specific reason, match the strain to
the evidence for that specific use — not the category. For anything beyond mild digestive
support, a conversation with a pharmacist or doctor about what's actually been studied
for your situation is worth more than the marketing on the box.
