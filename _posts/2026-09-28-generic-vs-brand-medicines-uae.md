A generic medicine contains the same active ingredient, at the same strength, as the brand-name version. Before it can be sold, it has to prove that it behaves the same way in your body. For most medicines, the differences you will notice are the price and how the tablet or box looks. A small group of medicines needs more care when you switch, and this guide explains which ones and why.

## What a generic medicine is

The first company to develop a medicine sells it under its own brand name. Other manufacturers can later make their own versions of the same medicine. These are generics.

The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) describes a generic as a medicine [created to be the same as an already marketed brand-name drug](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/generic-drugs/generic-drugs-questions-answers) in dosage form, safety, strength, route of administration, quality, performance and intended use. Abu Dhabi's Department of Health (DOH) uses similar wording. Its standard defines a generic as a product [usually intended to be interchangeable with the originator brand](https://www.doh.gov.ae/-/media/A7561F84C66D42199B2E7663DA4A8221.ashx), with the same active ingredient and proven bioequivalence.

In the UAE you may also hear generics called "similar" medicines. The Ministry of Health and Prevention (MOHAP) has described similar medicines as products that [contain the same active ingredients with the same concentration and pharmaceutical form](https://mohap.gov.ae/en/w/ministry-of-health-and-prevention-reduces-price-of-410-similar-drugs-in-line-with-government-directives) as the original.

Biological medicines such as insulins and antibody injections have their own copies, called biosimilars. They are not generics in the regulatory sense, and this article does not cover them.

## How bioequivalence is proven

A generic maker does not simply claim its product is the same. It has to show bioequivalence. In the FDA's words, this means the generic [delivers the same amount of active ingredient at the same rate and to the same extent](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/generic-drugs/generic-drugs-questions-answers) as the brand.

In a bioequivalence study, people take the generic and the brand, and blood tests track the medicine in their bloodstream. Two measures matter most. One is the total exposure over time (called AUC). The other is the highest level reached (called Cmax).

The researchers then calculate a 90% confidence interval for the ratio of generic to brand. This is the range in which the true difference very probably lies. Under the [GCC Guidelines for Bioequivalence](https://www.sfda.gov.sa/sites/default/files/2022-08/GCC_Guidelines_Bioequivalence31_0.pdf), the shared Gulf standard, that whole range must sit within 80.00% to 125.00% of the brand.

### The "20% weaker" myth

People often read the 80–125% window as permission for a generic to be up to 20% weaker or 25% stronger. That is a misreading. The rule applies to the whole confidence range, so a product has to sit close to the brand for every part of that range to fit inside the window.

Real data confirm this. An FDA team [reviewed 2,070 bioequivalence studies](https://www.natap.org/2016/HIV/AnnPharmacother2009-Davit1583-97.pdf) for generics approved between 1996 and 2007. The average difference from the brand was 3.56% for total exposure and 4.35% for peak level. In 97.6% of studies, the difference in total exposure was under 10%. The authors concluded that the FDA's criteria support approving generics that are therapeutically equivalent to the originals.

## What can be different

A generic has to match the brand on the things that make the medicine work. Other features can change.

| Must match the brand | Can differ from the brand |
|---|---|
| Active ingredient | Colour, shape and markings |
| Strength | Inactive ingredients (fillers, binders, colourings) |
| Dosage form (tablet, capsule, liquid) | Brand name and packaging |
| Route (by mouth, inhaled, on the skin) | Release technology in some modified-release products |
| Performance in the body, shown by bioequivalence | Design of devices such as inhalers and injectors |

The FDA says [minor differences in appearance, such as colour, do not affect how the medicine works](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/generic-drugs/generic-drugs-questions-answers). It also notes that in a minority of patients, inactive ingredients may affect tolerability or absorption.

Inactive ingredients are where a personal check helps. If you have lactose intolerance, coeliac disease or a reaction to certain colourings, ask your pharmacist to look at the leaflet of the new product. Its inactive ingredients may differ from the one you used before.

Devices and slow-release products also deserve attention. Abu Dhabi's standard [recommends brand-specific prescribing](https://www.doh.gov.ae/-/media/A7561F84C66D42199B2E7663DA4A8221.ashx) for certain modified-release medicines. It does the same for administration devices where the instructions differ, because technique affects how much medicine you actually receive. If your new inhaler or pen looks different, ask to be shown how to use it.

## What the clinical evidence shows

Bioequivalence studies measure blood levels. The next question is whether patients do equally well. A [2008 meta-analysis in JAMA](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK75738/) looked at 47 studies of cardiovascular medicines, 38 of them randomised trials. They covered nine drug classes, including beta-blockers, diuretics, statins, ACE inhibitors, antiplatelets and warfarin. The pooled result showed no significant difference between generic and brand. The authors wrote that the evidence "does not support the superiority of either drug class over the other."

## Why a generic can feel different

Expectations can shape how a medicine feels. This is the nocebo effect, where expecting harm makes side effects more likely to be noticed or reported.

In a [2025 randomised experiment](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12274324/), 184 healthy people took dummy pills that contained no medicine. Some pills carried brand-like cues and others generic-like cues, through the name or the price. People given the "generic" pills reported significantly more side effects. The effect worked through people's negative expectations.

This does not mean every problem after a switch is imagined. Some people do react to a particular product. Abu Dhabi's standard allows a doctor to specify the brand where a patient has a [documented allergy or adverse reaction to a particular generic](https://www.doh.gov.ae/-/media/A7561F84C66D42199B2E7663DA4A8221.ashx), or where treatment has failed. If something feels wrong after a change, tell your pharmacist or doctor rather than stopping the medicine.

## Medicines where switching needs care

Some medicines have a narrow therapeutic index. The FDA describes these as medicines with [little separation between therapeutic and toxic doses](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/cder-conversations/setting-and-implementing-standards-narrow-therapeutic-index-drugs), where levels that are slightly too low or too high can cause serious problems. Doses are often adjusted in very small steps, and blood levels are often monitored. The FDA names warfarin and levothyroxine as examples.

Regulators set stricter rules for these medicines. The GCC guideline tightens the bioequivalence window for total exposure to [90.00% to 111.11%](https://www.sfda.gov.sa/sites/default/files/2022-08/GCC_Guidelines_Bioequivalence31_0.pdf), and applies it to peak level too where that matters for safety. The FDA applies tighter criteria to these generics and still regards approved ones as substitutable for the brand.

Epilepsy medicines get particular attention. The UK's medicines regulator (MHRA) groups them into three categories, as [summarised by the NHS Specialist Pharmacy Service](https://sps.nhs.uk/articles/switching-brand-and-generic-anti-seizure-medicines-for-epilepsy/) in 2025:

- **Category 1.** Keep patients on the same manufacturer's product, switching only in exceptional situations such as shortages. Examples are carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbital and primidone.
- **Category 2.** Decide case by case, with the patient involved. Examples include lamotrigine, valproate, topiramate and clobazam.
- **Category 3.** Switching is usually acceptable. Examples include levetiracetam, gabapentin, pregabalin and lacosamide.

Abu Dhabi's standard lists narrow therapeutic index medicines among the cases where [brand-specific dispensing is recommended](https://www.doh.gov.ae/-/media/A7561F84C66D42199B2E7663DA4A8221.ashx). For long-term treatment, it asks pharmacists to stay as consistent as possible with one manufacturer.

If you take a medicine for epilepsy, your thyroid or blood thinning, or another medicine your doctor monitors closely, talk to your pharmacist or doctor before any switch. Do not change brand or manufacturer on your own. If a change does happen, your doctor may want to check how you are doing afterwards.

## How it works in the UAE

**The regulator.** Federal Decree-Law No. 28 of 2023 [created the Emirates Drug Establishment](https://uaelegislation.gov.ae/en/legislations/2122) (EDE), effective 30 September 2023. It [took over MOHAP's regulatory role](https://healthcarelifesciences.bakermckenzie.com/2024/02/21/uae-new-emirates-drug-corporation-soon-to-be-operational-replaces-mohap-as-federal-regulator-of-all-medical-products/) for medicines and medical devices. According to law firm Al Tamimi & Co., Federal Decree-Law No. 38 of 2024 on medical products [came into force on 2 January 2025](https://www.tamimi.com/news/the-new-era-of-pharmaceutical-regulation-in-the-uae-key-changes-under-federal-law-no-38-of-2024/), with EDE granting marketing authorisations. Our guide to [the UAE drug regulator and what changed](/blog/uae-drug-regulator-what-changed/) covers this in more detail.

**The bioequivalence standard.** The GCC guideline requires generics to be compared against [the original brand-name product](https://www.sfda.gov.sa/sites/default/files/2022-08/GCC_Guidelines_Bioequivalence31_0.pdf).

**Prices.** UAE authorities have cut medicine prices several times. In 2019, MOHAP Ministerial Decree No. 130 [cut the prices of 410 generic medicines](https://mohap.gov.ae/en/w/ministry-of-health-and-prevention-reduces-price-of-410-similar-drugs-in-line-with-government-directives) by 2% to 77%, and described them as equivalent in quality to the originals. MOHAP reported that 183 of those 410 were made locally. In May 2026, EDE [cut the prices of 168 medicines for chronic diseases](https://gulfnews.com/uae/health/uae-cuts-prices-of-168-medicines-for-chronic-diseases-by-up-to-60-per-cent-1.500553642) by 5% to nearly 60%, according to Gulf News. A lower price is not a sign of lower quality.

**Abu Dhabi's generic dispensing rule.** DOH's [Standard for the Prescribing and Dispensing of Generic Medicines](https://www.doh.gov.ae/-/media/A7561F84C66D42199B2E7663DA4A8221.ashx) (Version 2, March 2023) applies to DOH-licensed doctors and pharmacists. Doctors must prescribe by generic name. Pharmacists must dispense a generic against a generic-name prescription. There are exceptions, including narrow therapeutic index medicines, some modified-release products and devices, and biological medicines. Controlled medicines are prescribed by both brand name (where one exists) and generic name, and brand substitution needs the doctor's authorisation. Where a doctor has a clinical reason, they can write "Do Not Substitute" (DNS), and the pharmacist must then dispense that brand. When a different manufacturer's generic is supplied, the pharmacist should explain that the active substance remains the same.

**Dubai and the other emirates.** We could not find a published rule on pharmacist generic substitution from the Dubai Health Authority or at federal level. What you receive may depend on how your prescription is written and on your insurer's terms. If you are unsure, ask your pharmacist what they can supply and why.

## How to check you are getting the same medicine

- **Read the active ingredient and strength.** These are printed on the pack, usually under or near the brand name. If both match your previous medicine, you have the same medicine in a different box.
- **Check the form.** If your old pack said "modified-release", "SR", "XL" or similar, make sure the new one says the same.
- **Avoid doubling up.** The same ingredient can sit inside several brands. The NHS advises [not taking paracetamol with other medicines that contain paracetamol](https://www.nhs.uk/medicines/paracetamol-for-adults/), such as cold and flu remedies, because you may take too much. Tell your pharmacist about everything you take, including over-the-counter products.
- **Flag allergies and intolerances.** Mention any reaction to lactose, gluten or colourings each time your product changes.
- **Stay consistent where it matters.** For epilepsy, thyroid, blood-thinning and other closely monitored medicines, ask for the same manufacturer each time.

## Questions people ask

**Can a pharmacy give me a generic instead of the brand on my prescription?**
In Abu Dhabi, a prescription written by generic name is dispensed as a generic, unless an exception applies or the doctor has written "Do Not Substitute". Elsewhere in the UAE no published rule could be found, so ask your pharmacist.

**Are generic drugs as good as name-brand drugs?**
For most medicines, yes. They must pass bioequivalence testing, and a large review of cardiovascular studies found no difference in outcomes. Take extra care with narrow therapeutic index medicines.

**Why does my tablet look different this month?**
Your pharmacy may have supplied a different manufacturer's version. Check that the active ingredient and strength match. If they do, the medicine is the same. If they don't, ask before taking it.

**Can I ask for the brand instead?**
You can ask your pharmacist. If you have had a documented reaction to a particular generic, your doctor can specify the brand. Coverage may depend on your insurer, so check at the counter.
